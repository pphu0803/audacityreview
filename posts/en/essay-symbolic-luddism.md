## A Respectable Ban

In October 2026, Nathan J. Robinson, editor of the left-wing magazine *Current Affairs*, published an editorial announcement: the magazine's newly signed union contract contained one of the strictest anti-AI policies in publishing. No AI-generated text would be printed, no AI-generated images used. Open the magazine and you would read the thoughts of humans, see the work of human hands.

The statement is candid, even moving. Robinson admits he once played around with DALL-E himself. He "forgot for a moment that we live under capitalism, where every new technology will be used by the ruling class to fuck over workers." He invokes John Henry from the folk ballad. Before we let that steam drill beat us, we will die with our hammer in our hand.

As a fellow editor, I respect the choice. But there is a less respectable fact to face: this is a textbook gesture of neo-Luddism, and it fails precisely on the "neo". It has turned Luddism into a symbol, and symbols cannot smash machines.

## I. What Made the Original Luddism Strong

For two hundred years, "Luddite" has been a byword for ignorance and backwardness. That is history written by the victors.

The real Luddite movement was not a panic-stricken riot against machinery but highly organized labor action. The framework knitters smashed the specific machines that particular employers had brought in to depress piece rates, then melted back underground, forcing the employers back to the bargaining table. Destruction was the means; leverage was the end.

The entire strength of the original Luddism lay here: its action was physical and concrete, capable of halting production directly and forcing capital to pay a price. The hammer falls, the frames stop, the ledger bleeds. Capital must respond.

Nor are the people at *Current Affairs* ignorant of this history: they published "The Luddites Were Right" back in 2021, and the new statement links to it. And precisely because they are not ignorant, the choice demands questioning: knowing full well that the Luddite movement in the flesh was destructive bargaining, why offer instead a non-destructive declaration?

## II. Contract Clauses Cannot Smash Machines

The answer is not complicated: a unionized small nonprofit magazine never had a hammer in the first place. AI's productivity explosion lives in Silicon Valley's server clusters, in the hundred-billion-dollar training pipelines. One magazine announcing that it will not use AI applies exactly zero drag to that pipeline. It interrupts no production; it forces no capital to pay any price.

So the ban's actual force can only land in two places.

The first is internal: binding their own editors and freelancers (moral self-discipline). The second is the market: offering a quality guarantee to the small cohort of readers willing to pay a premium for "handmade thought" (niche positioning).

We have seen where the second road leads. Organic food, handmade leather goods, craft beer: all traveled the same trajectory, from critique of industrial production to premium category on the shelf. "Our content is all-natural, AI-free" is structurally identical to "no pesticide residue in this product."

A product of the critique of capitalism ends up as a new category within capitalism's vertical markets. This is not failure, it is co-optation, and co-optation of the most willing kind.

## III. The Price of Divided Theory and Practice

Some will say: a small shop cannot bring down big capital. Surely it may at least take a stand?

Of course it may. The problem is that the more penetrating the left media's diagnosis of AI capitalism, the wider the gap when the prescription turns out to be "our magazine won't use it." Readers take in the *Futurism* reporting. More than 200,000 creative-industry jobs lost in four years. Only to discover that the form of resistance on offer is a subscription to a clean magazine.

That gap feeds not militancy but fatalism: so all we can do is not buy. So the most dignified life available to the fish, between the knife and the chopping board, is picking a patch of seaweed the algorithm hasn't touched.

This is the greatest danger of symbolic resistance. It does not frighten the enemy; it corrodes the confidence of one's own side.

## IV. Where the Real Leverage Lies

Set beside it another labor action from the same period. The 2023 Hollywood writers' strike did not end in "ban AI" but in bringing AI under labor control. Companies could not force writers to use AI, AI-generated material could not be treated as source material, and human writers' credit and compensation were written into the contract.

This is precisely the direction argued in the "means of cognitive production" trilogy. Compute, data, and weights constitute the means of cognitive production of the AI age, and their returns are undergoing a phase-transition-like concentration into the hands of a vanishingly small class of controllers. The corresponding action is not retreat into the artisanal past but the socialization of algorithmic rents, the common ownership of training data, collective bargaining over digital infrastructure. That road is hard, but at least it aims at the relations of production themselves, not at consumer choice.

*Current Affairs*'s John Henry, in the end, died beside the steam drill, and the ballad does not lie: individual heroism cannot beat the machine. Real victory came from swapping the hammer for organization: unions, contracts, bargaining power. The things that point toward ownership.

The soul of Luddism was never in the hammer; it was in making capital pay. The hammer was merely that era's carrier. What this era's carrier might be: that is the real question. The innocence of one magazine is not the answer.

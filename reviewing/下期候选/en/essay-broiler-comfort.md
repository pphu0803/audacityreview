## I. An Indelicate Metaphor

Start with a biological fact: the most numerous bird in the world is, in all likelihood, not the sparrow but the broiler chicken on the industrial farm — tens of billions of them are slaughtered every year.

The broiler's life is not actually bad. A climate-controlled shed, feed delivered on schedule, no predators, no weather to speak of. Compared with a sparrow scratching for food in a winter wind, the average broiler lives far more comfortably and withstands risk far better — when avian flu arrives, the farm vaccinates; when a cold snap hits, the shed has heating.

There is only one price: it can no longer fly. And with each generation of selective breeding, the breed grows more uniform.

The metaphor is indelicate, but applied to Western societies today it is remarkably precise.

## II. The Economics of Captivity

The sparrow's life is classical capitalism: workers who can starve at any moment will resist, will organize, will produce genuine movements — the Luddites were sparrows smashing the equipment of the coop. The broiler's life is welfare capitalism plus a mature consumer apparatus: cheap streaming, takeout on demand, consumer goods on installment plans, a social safety net underneath it all. The feed is not luxurious, but it never runs out.

The decisive change lies not in the quantity of feed but in the structure of possibility. A chicken accustomed to a climate-controlled shed does not set about demolishing the shed — not because it loves the shed, but because if the shed collapses, what awaits it is not the open sky of freedom but the very elements it has lost the capacity to endure.

The obedience bred by captivity is physiological, not the product of ideological instruction. It is sturdier than any propaganda.

Herein lies the reason mass movements struggle to appear. It is not that people cannot see the fence — they see it perfectly well, and tacitly steer around it in public discussion. It is that tearing the fence down never pays in any serious calculation: present comfort is certain, and the price of freedom is unpayable.

## III. The Monoculture of Thought

The second consequence of broilerization is subtler: the monoculture of the breed corresponds, at the level of society, to the rigor mortis of thought.

The "pluralism" the West prides itself on is degrading into minor variation within one grand frame. Politically, the competition between the two parties increasingly resembles Coke versus Pepsi: the packaging is distinct, the formula nearly identical, and whichever side takes power, the interest structure of Wall Street, the military-industrial complex, and the tech giants does not move an inch. Culturally, the permitted radical causes overlap to a striking degree; the spectrum of opinion looks broad, but every end of it is tethered to the same stake.

Monoculture is efficiency on the farm; in the marketplace of ideas it is brittleness. Without genuine heterogeneity there is no genuine alternative; and a society without alternatives, when confronted with events outside its frame, cannot even imagine them.

## IV. Stiffening in Comfort?

The judgment is pessimistic: there will be no mass movement. They will live comfortably, and may go on living comfortably indefinitely — at least so long as the power stays on in the shed.

But the judgment carries its own suspense. The stability of the farm system presupposes a controllable environment: feed prices, temperature, vaccine supply. When a genuinely exogenous shock arrives — an irreversible depression, or a drastic rearrangement of the global geopolitical order — what becomes of a system lacking "genetic diversity"? Does it display the resilience of redundancy, or snap overnight because it holds no reserve of adaptation whatsoever?

Empires in history, once calcified, were seldom overthrown by internal uprising; they were swept away by external changes they had never imagined. The broiler has no reason to worry about this, because the broiler does not need history. Human beings do.

There is no hurry to give an answer. One observation will suffice: when a society's stability is purchased at the price of intellectual monoculture, then that stability itself is the society's deepest instability.

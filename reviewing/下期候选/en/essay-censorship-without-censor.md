## Prologue: The Canary in the Mine

The canary was the alarm the miners carried down the shaft. On 19 October 2026, the British left-wing news site The Canary announced that it was closing for the foreseeable future. Eleven years, and that was that.

The canary in the mine is dead.

The death certificate reads cleanly: in late June, Lloyds Bank withheld a substantial amount of its money and offered no reason; in early September, Metro Bank shut its other account, again without a reason. The pay stopped going out, operations seized up, the site went dark.

The Canary had broken no law. And along the whole chain that killed it, not one link was under any legal duty to give a substantive reason or submit to review. And that is the most important thing about the entire affair.

On the material publicly available, this may be the first media organisation in the West to be crushed by debanking. The cutoff may not have been the only cause of death, but it was very likely the final blow.

## I. Where Is the Censor?

We are used to imagining free speech as a defensive line: the state may not punish you for what you say. The line is real, but it faces one way only. The grammar of the First Amendment is "Congress shall make no law": it binds the state and says not one word about private power.

The Canary was not killed by the state. It was killed by two banks, by the payment rails, by those days when the wages could not be paid. Nobody along that chain made a "political decision"; every one of them made a business judgment.

Set the comparison beside it and the thing becomes clear. In 2010 WikiLeaks had its donation channels cut off by Visa, Mastercard and PayPal, and by its own account lost some 95 per cent of its donation income; it survived, because other routes were still open to it.

When an outlet is pushed out of the payment system by two banks at once, it cannot even perform the act of receiving money. It does not need to be forbidden to speak. It only needs to go unpaid.

"No reason given" is not a procedural defect; it is the core of the mechanism. State censorship at least hands you an object for your hatred, an appeal you can attempt, a name you can put in an editorial. Here there is not even a defendant.

The system is maximally lenient at its centre and maximally harsh at its edge. You may curse the president as you please. But let your survival depend on the payment and advertising infrastructure, and your existence can be cancelled by a single line of compliance judgment.

## II. The Word "Risk"

Lloyds gave no reason, and "risk appetite" is precisely the category of legitimate reason that Parliament accepts in the industry-wide account-closure statistics. Here is exclusion at its most elegant. It is unfalsifiable: you cannot prove "we do not want your politics". It is unexaminable: due process does not apply to commercial decisions. And it is self-executing; no enforcement agency is required. Freeze the account, and the staff clear out on their own.

Its genius is the translation of a political judgment into a technical one. Advertisers withdrawing their money is not political pressure but "brand safety"; the campaign against you is not a partisan purge but "countering fake news"; the bank closing your account is not a verdict on your opinions but "compliance". Once the translation is complete, the argument is over. You cannot put "risk appetite" in the witness box.

The Canary's prehistory runs precisely along these rails. It was founded in 2015 on the crest of Corbyn's election, and at one point it drew 8.5 million hits a month. The confidential papers of Labour Together, the think tank of the Labour right, set out the objective in so many words: "to delegitimise and destroy" Corbyn.

The instrument was Stop Funding Fake News (SFFN), a campaign launched by the same people who went on to found the Centre for Countering Digital Hate (CCDH, now a US-registered nonprofit).

Morgan McSweeney, later Keir Starmer's Downing Street chief of staff, secretly recruited the television presenter Rachel Riley as the public face; through SFFN and the anti-Corbyn Jewish Labour Movement, they pressed The Canary's advertisers.

The Canary was eventually cleared by Impress, the independent press regulator, but the advertisers were long gone. On SFFN's own boast, The Canary "went down from 22 staff to one member of staff within a few months of us targeting it".

Not one link in the chain admits to doing politics. The think tank does research, the nonprofit fights fake news, the advertisers protect the brand, the bank manages risk. Each performs its function; the loop closes.

And there is one more layer. Former employees, speaking to The Guardian, said the real cause was "wider financial mismanagement". Of the £2 million raised last year only £400,000 remained by the summer, burnt on a short-lived print paper and on the acquisition of other sites. This may well be true.

But attend to the structure. In a world without written reasons, without hearings, without disclosure, there is no telling genuinely bad management from manufactured mismanagement; whichever is true, the final blow was the same cutoff. Even the deceased cannot name its killer; that is why the machine runs so clean.

## III. One Question Dismantles the Entire Foundation

Liberalism runs out of road here. It only knows how to ask: has the state overstepped? And the state never lifted a hand.

Ask a different question: whose protection does private property enjoy, and against whom?

The state's: enforced by courts, police and bailiffs. Property is not a natural right that predates the state. It is a set of relations that the state defines and upholds by force: what counts as possession, what counts as trespass, how a debt is enforced, how limited liability carves up risk. Without that apparatus of coercion, property is merely a phrase.

So the state is not capital's external constraint. The state is capital's form of existence.

This also explains the most awkward feature of the Canary affair: Lloyds's freedom to close the account is itself a property freedom, protected by the same state; The Canary's freedom of speech is likewise protected by that state. When the two collided, the legal form chose the former — not because anyone gave an order, but because the power of property runs by itself, while relief for speech waits for someone to reach out a hand.

The asymmetry requires no political decision; it is written into the structure of the statutes. And once the axis of "state versus individual" is bolted into place, the side of capital goes invisible on its own: that "individual" is a legal person whose property is a creature of the state's own definition.

## IV. Sovereignty Could Once Be Delegated

History sees it more plainly. Sovereignty could once be rented out: the East India Company kept an army, minted its own coin, ran its own courts, and collected taxes directly in Bengal. It was not a "regulated company"; it was an entity holding sovereignty by charter. One of the great labours of the nineteenth-century nation-state was taking that rented sovereignty back.

What is happening now is a re-delegation of it, carried out in the name of "property". Investor arbitration lets capital sue states before private tribunals whose awards sit beyond the appellate reach of domestic courts; the payment and clearing system carries no public-service obligation; the platforms replace administrative law with "community standards".

Where central capacity runs thin, the cartels and the private armies take over taxation, policing and the settlement of disputes directly.

These delegations have one thing in common: they all present the legal appearance of "exercising property". Governance is redescribed as "what is mine is mine to command". So there is no need for legislation, none for public reasons, none for proportionality review.

And incidentally, these are not one thing. Market power, chokepoint power (control of the passages others have no choice but to pass through), and coercive power are three different mechanisms.

The Canary died of the second; the cartel belongs to the third; the media empire is a compound of the first two. What it controls is more than a market: it controls the informational conditions under which political legitimacy is manufactured. And the second is the most underestimated of the three, because it does not look like an empire. It looks like a bank.

## V. We Once Had the Vocabulary

"Who governs them" once had an answer.

The common law holds a concept, the common carrier: whoever controls a passage that others must use bears the duty to serve all comers without discrimination. Railways, telegraph and telephone were all brought under it.

Munn v. Illinois, in 1877, laid down a principle: private property, once "affected with a public interest", ceases to be merely private and falls under public regulation. Banking and clearing, historically, were treated as trades "affected with a public interest" too.

That vocabulary existed, and then it vanished from the language of regulation. This was no natural evolution: "infrastructure" was redescribed as "services", "public obligations" as "commercial choices", "regulation" as "interference in the market". Lose the word that names the nature of a power, and you lose the ability to put obligations on it.

It is not that the language never came back. New UK rules in force since April 2026 require banks to give ninety days' notice of a closure and to state their reasons in writing; but a single exemption for suspected financial crime swallows both obligations whole.

The Canary died after those rules took effect: no notice, no reasons; whether that is unlawful is exactly the question this affair leaves open. And even if the rules were enforced to the letter, no law on the books can now say: you control a payment channel, therefore you must give substantive reasons and submit to review. That sentence was deleted long ago.

## VI. Silence Is an Act of Sovereignty Too

Someone will say: the state is powerless here; capital is simply too big.

No. The state holds every instrument it needs: a regulator could enforce the written-reasons duty notwithstanding the crime exemption, could find the treatment unfair; Parliament could legislate payment and clearing into a public-service obligation. None of this happened.

And doing nothing is itself an act of state. A sovereign that chooses not to ask questions is underwriting the banks. The Canary was not killed by an invisible hand. It was killed by a hand that chose silence.

Nor is this an isolated case. After Nigel Farage was debanked by Coutts in 2023, a parliamentary examination of FCA data found more than 140,000 business accounts closed by the banks in a single year. The government's subsequent inquiry concluded that the closures had nothing to do with politics. Judge that conclusion as you see fit.

This July, UK Finance, the banking lobby, was building a cross-bank platform to share information on customers carrying "markers of economic crime". A person flagged by one bank could find the entire industry closed to them.

In the United States, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency reviewed the nine largest banks. It found that between 2020 and September 2025 all nine carried policies capable of letting them deny or restrict service on grounds of political or religious belief. The Cato Institute, for its part, criticised the review as thin on evidence, too thin to establish systematic discrimination.

And a presidential memorandum has already written "debank" into the list of actions to be taken against the so-called "political terrorists".

The particular death The Canary died is the product of a distinctly British gap; but "distinct" is not "exceptional": it is this grammar, demonstrated in full, in one concrete jurisdiction.

## Coda: Who Decides the Exception

There was never any such thing as free speech in itself — it is a legal form, and whom it binds and whom it spares is written into the structure of the statutes, not into slogans.

The real question is: who decides the exception, and whether the people it is decided upon are permitted to ask why.

For The Canary the answer was no. No law requires anyone to give a substantive reason for what was done; even whether anyone broke a rule is something the dead must fight to establish for themselves.
